Buying criteria: what to look for in a wrinkle cream

1. Ingredients

Check the first five to ten ingredients — these make up the bulk of what you are applying. Look for evidence-backed actives relevant to your concern: retinol, peptides, hyaluronic acid, niacinamide, ceramides, vitamin C. Be cautious of products where active ingredients appear at the very bottom of a long list — concentration matters.

2. Skin type match

A rich cream suitable for dry, mature skin may be too heavy for oily or combination skin. A lightweight gel formulation may not provide enough moisture for very dry skin. Match the texture and ingredients to your skin type.

3. Irritation risk

Fragrance is one of the most common causes of skincare reactions. If you have sensitive skin, look for fragrance-free formulas. High concentrations of active ingredients can also cause initial irritation — especially retinol. Lower concentrations with gradual introduction reduce this risk.

4. Texture and feel

How a product feels on the skin affects how consistently it gets used. The best product for your skin is one you will actually use every day. Consider whether you prefer a light fluid, a rich cream, a gel-cream, or a balm texture.

5. SPF

Some moisturisers include SPF. This is a genuine benefit — fewer steps in a morning routine makes consistency easier. However, ensure the SPF level is adequate (SPF 30 minimum, ideally SPF 50) and that you are applying enough product to achieve the stated SPF.

6. Fragrance

Fragrance (listed as "parfum" on UK and EU labels, or "fragrance" in the US) is a common irritant, particularly for sensitive or mature skin. Choosing fragrance-free products reduces irritation risk significantly.

7. Price and value

Price does not correlate with efficacy. A well-formulated mid-range product may outperform an expensive cream with a poor formulation. However, very cheap products sometimes use lower-quality raw materials or lower active concentrations. We assess value by looking at what the formulation actually contains relative to its price.

8. Packaging

Certain ingredients — particularly vitamin C and retinol — can degrade when exposed to light and air. Look for opaque packaging and pumps or sealed tubes rather than open jars when buying products with these actives.

9. Evidence

Be cautious of vague claims. "Visibly reduces wrinkles" could mean almost anything. Look for products where the active ingredients (not just the formula as a whole) have credible published research behind them. Our ingredient guides explain what the research currently says for each major ingredient.

10. User suitability

Consider your age, skin type, concerns, budget, and lifestyle. There is no single best wrinkle cream — only the best wrinkle cream for your specific skin at this point in your life.

Quick tips before you buy

  • Check the ingredients list — not just the marketing claims on the front of the packaging
  • Choose based on your skin type, not just your age or concern
  • Fragrance-free is usually a safer default, especially if you have sensitive skin
  • Give a product at least 8–12 weeks of consistent daily use before judging it
  • Only introduce one new product at a time, so you know what is working
  • Always use SPF in the morning — it matters more than any single wrinkle cream
  • If you have a reaction, stop using the product and let your skin recover before trying another
  • Price is not a reliable guide to quality — look at the formulation, not the label

Frequently asked questions

  • Look at the INCI ingredients list, not the marketing copy. The best wrinkle creams contain evidence-backed actives — retinol (or retinyl palmitate for a gentler option), peptides, hyaluronic acid, and ceramides. Key actives should appear in the first half of the ingredients list. Avoid products where actives are buried at the bottom — they are likely present in too small an amount to be effective.

  • Both serve different purposes. A day cream should be lighter and must include or be followed by SPF. A night cream can be richer and may contain retinol (which should only be used at night). You do not need both if your moisturiser is suitable for both day and night use, though most people benefit from slightly different textures.

  • Good value means the product contains meaningful concentrations of evidence-backed actives in formulations that suit your skin type — not the cheapest price or the most expensive label. A mid-range retinol cream with ceramides and no fragrance outperforms an expensive cream with fragrance and no proven actives.

  • Give any new skincare product at least 12 weeks of consistent daily use before assessing results. Skin cell turnover takes roughly 4–6 weeks, and ingredients like retinol require several cycles to show visible improvement. An exception: if you experience irritation or a reaction, stop sooner and assess.

  • You can layer products, but introducing them one at a time is safer. Start with a basic routine and add actives one by one with 2–4 weeks between introductions. Using multiple active ingredients at once — high-concentration retinol, AHAs, and vitamin C simultaneously — increases the risk of irritation.
