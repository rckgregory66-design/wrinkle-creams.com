Retinol creams for wrinkles — a plain English guide

Retinol is one of the most researched skincare ingredients for fine lines and texture. But it needs to be introduced carefully. This guide explains what it is, how to use it, and what to expect.

Retinol and pregnancy

Retinol and all retinoids should be avoided during pregnancy and while breastfeeding. If you are pregnant, trying to conceive, or breastfeeding, please speak to your doctor or midwife before using any vitamin A derivative. This guide covers over-the-counter retinol only — not prescription retinoids like tretinoin.

What is retinol?

Retinol is a form of vitamin A — specifically a retinoid that can be converted by the skin into retinoic acid, the active form that influences skin cell behaviour. It is available over the counter in moisturisers, serums, and dedicated retinol creams at concentrations typically ranging from 0.025% to 1%.

Prescription-strength retinoids (such as tretinoin/retinoic acid) work more directly and are more potent, but they require a prescription and medical supervision. This guide covers over-the-counter retinol products only.

Why do people use retinol for fine lines and texture?

Retinol encourages faster skin cell turnover — the process by which old skin cells are shed and replaced with new ones. With consistent use over several months, this can lead to improvements in:

  • The appearance of fine lines and shallow wrinkles
  • Skin texture — reduced roughness or unevenness
  • Skin tone — a more even, less dull appearance

The evidence for retinol in these areas is stronger than for most other over-the-counter skincare ingredients. However, it is not a quick fix, and results require consistent use over months, not days.

How to start with retinol — slowly

The most common mistake with retinol is starting too often and at too high a concentration. Retinol causes what is often called the "retinol adjustment period" — a phase of dryness, flaking, redness, or sensitivity as the skin adapts. This does not mean retinol is damaging your skin, but it should be managed carefully.

  1. Start low

    Choose a product with a concentration of 0.025% to 0.1% to begin with. Lower concentrations are gentler and still effective over time.

  2. Start once a week

    Apply your retinol product on just one evening per week to begin with. After 2–4 weeks with no reaction, try twice a week. Continue building slowly over months.

  3. Apply to dry skin

    Apply retinol to fully dry skin (wait 10–20 minutes after cleansing). Wet skin can increase penetration and irritation risk.

  4. Use a buffer moisturiser if needed

    If irritation occurs, try applying a plain moisturiser before your retinol (sometimes called "sandwiching") to reduce the rate of absorption. This helps very sensitive or dry skin tolerate retinol better.

  5. Always use SPF the next morning

    Retinol increases photosensitivity — your skin will be more vulnerable to UV damage the morning after use. Daily SPF is essential when using retinol.

Common irritation signs — when to reduce

Normal adjustment vs. genuine reaction

Some dryness, mild flaking, and temporary redness during the first few weeks of retinol use is normal adjustment. This typically settles as your skin adapts. A genuine reaction — sustained burning, severe peeling, hive-like rash, or swelling — is different and means you should stop using the product.

Reduce your frequency or concentration if you experience:

  • Persistent redness that does not settle
  • Stinging or burning that lasts more than a few minutes after application
  • Severe dryness or tight, uncomfortable skin
  • Flaking that is extensive rather than mild

If symptoms persist after reducing frequency, stop using the product and let your skin recover. Speak to a dermatologist if you are concerned about your reaction.

What not to mix without care

Some ingredient combinations can increase irritation when used at the same time or on the same night:

  • Retinol + AHAs or BHAs: Both are exfoliating. Using them together, especially at higher concentrations, can cause significant irritation. Use on alternate evenings if you want to include both.
  • Retinol + vitamin C: Traditionally advised against on the same application because they have different optimal pH ranges. A safer approach is vitamin C in the morning and retinol at night.
  • Retinol + benzoyl peroxide: Can deactivate each other and increase dryness. Use separately.

Niacinamide, hyaluronic acid, ceramides, and peptides are all generally compatible with retinol.

Retinol cream buying checklist

  • Start with 0.025%–0.1% concentration if you are new to retinol
  • Choose opaque, airtight packaging (tube or pump) — retinol degrades in light and air
  • Fragrance-free is safer, especially for sensitive skin
  • Look for supporting ingredients: hyaluronic acid, ceramides, or glycerin to offset dryness
  • Avoid products that combine retinol with AHAs unless you are experienced with both
  • Check the ingredients list — retinol should appear in the first half, not buried at the bottom
  • Evening use only — retinol breaks down in sunlight and should not be used as a daytime product
  • Have a plain, fragrance-free moisturiser on hand to buffer on the nights you apply it
